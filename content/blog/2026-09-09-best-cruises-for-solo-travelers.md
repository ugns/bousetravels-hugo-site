Cruising solo is no longer a niche. Cruise lines have spent the last decade building studio cabins, host programs, and social spaces specifically for travelers who book as one. The experience can be one of the most confident ways to see the world on your own terms—but only if the ship actually supports it.

The March post on this site covered *why* cruises work for independent travelers. This one is about *which* cruises to book.

## What Makes a Cruise Genuinely Solo-Friendly

Not every ship is equally good for one person. Look for:

- **Studio or single-occupancy cabins.** These remove the second-person fare that can double the cost of a standard stateroom.
- **A solo traveler program.** Hosted mixers, dedicated lounges, and optional dining companions matter more than most people expect on night one.
- **A ship size that matches your energy.** Mega-ships give you anonymity and activity. Smaller ships make it easier to meet the same people twice.
- **An itinerary with easy port days.** Independent travelers do best when they can walk off the ship and explore without a group.

If a line still prices every cabin as if two people are sailing, the math gets hard fast. Ask about single supplements before you fall in love with a sailing.

## Strong Options by Travel Style

**Norwegian Cruise Line (studio cabins and Studio Lounge)**
Norwegian was early to treat solo guests as a real market. Studio cabins on several ships, plus a key-access Studio Lounge with hosted events, give independent travelers a built-in social base without forcing participation.

**Virgin Voyages (adults-only, no single supplement on many sailings)**
Virgin's 18+ ships, flexible dining, and frequently waived single supplements make it a standout for adults traveling alone. The onboard culture is social without being formal.

**Royal Caribbean (scale and activity)**
If you want a ship that never runs out of things to do, Royal Caribbean's larger vessels work well. You can be as private or as involved as you like. Solo-specific cabins are less common, so compare the true occupancy cost carefully.

**Holland America and Cunard (quieter, enrichment-forward)**
Travelers who prefer lectures, live music, and a calmer evening rhythm often do better here than on a high-energy family ship. These lines attract an adult demographic and a more conversational onboard culture.

**River cruises (Europe in particular)**
A 150-passenger river ship is one of the easiest places to travel alone. Meals are communal, shore excursions are included, and you are never far from help. Single occupancy pricing varies—some river lines run periodic solo promotions that are worth watching.

## Itineraries That Work Especially Well Solo

- **Mediterranean and Greek Isles:** Walkable ports, café culture, and short tender or dock times.
- **Alaska:** Daytime scenery does the entertaining; evenings stay unhurried.
- **Northern Europe and the British Isles:** Strong enrichment programming and compact port towns.
- **Caribbean on a shorter sailing:** A useful first solo cruise if you want to test the format before a longer voyage.

Avoid itineraries that require complicated independent transfers in every port unless you enjoy that kind of logistics. The point of solo cruising is freedom, not a second job.

## Cabin and Cost Decisions That Matter

A studio inside cabin on the right ship often beats a double-occupancy balcony with a 100% single supplement. Location still matters: mid-ship, middle deck, away from late-night venues if you want quiet.

Confirm:
- Whether the single supplement is waived or reduced
- Whether studio cabins include the same perks as other categories
- Dining policies for a table of one (most ships accommodate this easily)

## Expert Insight

The best solo cruise is the one that matches how you actually spend a vacation—not how you think you *should* spend it. Travelers who want conversation every night need a different ship from travelers who want a balcony, a book, and room service. Both are valid. The mismatch is what makes a sailing feel lonely.

## Final Thoughts

Solo cruising works when the product is designed for one person, not merely tolerated. Choose the cabin math, the social temperature, and the itinerary with the same care you would give a destination. Done well, it is one of the most independent—and least isolated—ways to travel.
